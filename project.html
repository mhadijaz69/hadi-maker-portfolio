01 — THE IDEA

Why I built it

The Problem

Aviation emissions are often presented as large, abstract figures that are difficult to connect to an individual flight or aircraft choice. I became interested in whether these calculations could be made more accessible while still being grounded in published aviation research.

The Goal

I wanted to create a tool that could take technical ideas from my aviation-emissions research and turn them into an interactive application that lets users explore how route and aircraft selection influence estimated environmental and operational outcomes.

02 — HOW IT WORKS

From route selection to comparison

01

Select a route

Users choose origin and destination airports. Geographic coordinates are used to calculate route distance.

02

Choose aircraft

Airline and aircraft selections connect the route to aircraft-specific performance information used by the estimator.

03

Estimate impact

The application estimates fuel consumption, carbon emissions, operating fuel cost, and per-passenger impact.

04

Compare aircraft

Users can compare several aircraft on the same route and explore differences in efficiency, emissions, and estimated cost.

03 — RESEARCH FOUNDATION

The project began as research.

Before building the application, I conducted a ten-week research project through the RISE Research Scholar Programme under the one-on-one mentorship of Yuiko Ichikawa, PhD in Meteorology from the University of Hokkaido and MPhil in Data Intensive Science from the University of Cambridge.

I studied aviation emissions, fuel-consumption modelling, ICAO emissions factors, QAR, ADS-B and simulated-flight-data methodologies, regression-based approaches, and the limitations of publicly accessible aviation datasets.

My original research direction involved a more complex artificial-intelligence approach. However, the available public data did not contain enough consistent information on passenger loads, flight distances, and fuel consumption to support the model reliably.

I therefore shifted toward a more transparent regression-based approach. That decision taught me that selecting an appropriate method is more important than selecting the most complicated one.

07 — ENGINEERING DECISIONS

Challenges, limitations & decisions

Data availability

Detailed operational airline data is not always publicly available. I had to work with accessible aircraft specifications and published research while recognising where assumptions were necessary.

Changing the model

I initially considered an AI-based approach, but changed direction when the available dataset did not adequately support it. This resulted in a more transparent and defensible methodology.

Accuracy vs accessibility

A professional airline operations model can require data unavailable to a public application. I focused on creating an understandable estimator while documenting the assumptions behind its outputs.

Turning research into software

A methodology that works on paper still needs to be translated into inputs, calculations, validation, visualisations, and a user interface before it becomes useful software.

Model limitation

The calculator is an educational estimation tool rather than a certified airline operational or regulatory emissions model. Real flights are affected by factors including weather, routing, payload, engine variant, altitude, taxi time, and operational procedures.

08 — NEXT STEPS

How I would develop it further

Broader datasets

Expand the airport, airline, and aircraft databases to support a much larger set of international routes.

More detailed fuel modelling

Incorporate richer flight-phase and aircraft performance data where reliable public datasets become available.

Live economic data

Replace simplified fuel-cost assumptions with a reliable live or regularly updated aviation fuel pricing source.

Model uncertainty

Add uncertainty ranges and clearer sensitivity analysis so users can understand how assumptions influence the final estimate.

09 — REFLECTION

What I learned

The most important lesson from this project was that engineering is not simply about producing a calculation. It requires deciding what information can be trusted, recognising the limitations of a model, communicating assumptions clearly, and repeatedly refining a solution when new problems appear.

Moving from research to a functioning web application also showed me how programming can turn theoretical analysis into something people can interact with and question. That combination of engineering, computation, and real-world application is what I hope to continue exploring in college.
